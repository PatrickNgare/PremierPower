Most problems on an electrical installation are decided long before the first cable is pulled: a supply applied for too late, a distribution board with no spare ways, a cable route that clashes with the ductwork. Planning in the right order avoids them. This is the sequence we recommend for a new building or a major refurbishment.

## 1. Start with how the building will be used

Everything else follows from use. A hotel, an office floor, a clinic and an apartment block have very different loads, different patterns of use across the day, and different consequences when power is lost. Write down early:

- what the spaces are for, and how many people use them;
- which equipment is essential — kitchens, lifts, medical equipment, servers, pumps;
- what must keep running in a power cut, and for how long;
- likely future additions — more floors, electric vehicle charging, solar.

## 2. Estimate the load and agree the supply

From that use, the design engineer estimates the maximum demand — the load the building will actually draw at its busiest, not the sum of every appliance. That figure sets the size of the incoming supply. For larger buildings the supply application to the utility, and any transformer it requires, can take months, so it should be started as soon as the demand is known.

## 3. Design the distribution

With the supply settled, the design lays out how power reaches each area: the main switchboard, sub-main cables, distribution boards on each floor or zone, and the final circuits. Good design at this stage:

- separates essential and non-essential loads, so standby power only carries what it must;
- leaves spare capacity and spare ways in every board for later changes;
- places boards where they can be reached for maintenance;
- coordinates protective devices so a fault trips only the circuit it is on, not the whole floor.

## 4. Decide on standby and protection

Decide early whether the building needs a standby generator, UPS for critical equipment, surge protection, and — increasingly — solar generation tied into the distribution. Each affects space, ventilation, cable routes and the design of the boards, and is far cheaper to plan in than to add.

## 5. Coordinate with the rest of the design

Electrical routes share ceiling voids, risers and walls with plumbing, ventilation, data, security and fire systems. Agree routes on the drawings before construction, and agree the programme with the main contractor: when first-fix conduit must be in before slabs are cast or walls closed, and when second fix can follow finishes.

## 6. Plan for inspection, testing and handover

Testing is part of the programme, not something squeezed in before opening. Allow time to inspect and test each stage before it is covered, and to commission the main systems under load at the end. Agree from the start what documents will be handed over — test certificate, as-built drawings, board schedules, equipment manuals. See [what testing and commissioning involves](/insights/understanding-electrical-testing-and-commissioning).

## 7. Choose the contractor with the plan in hand

With use, load, distribution and programme written down, quotations can be compared like for like, and the contractor can price the job that will actually be built. See [how to choose an electrical contractor](/insights/how-to-choose-an-electrical-contractor-in-kenya).
