Choosing the electrical contractor for a building is one of the few decisions on a project that is still visible twenty years later — in the tripping that does or does not happen, in the bills, and in whether the installation passes its next inspection. It is also one that is often made on price alone. This guide sets out what to check instead.

## 1. Check both registrations, not one

In Kenya, two different bodies are involved, and they cover different things.

- **The Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA)** licenses electrical contractors and electricians to carry out electrical installation work. A licensed contractor holds a class of licence that sets the size and type of installation it may work on.
- **The National Construction Authority (NCA)** registers contractors working on construction projects, in categories that reflect the value of work they may take on and the specialism — for example, electrical engineering services.

For anything beyond a small domestic job, ask for both, and check the class or category against the size of your project. A contractor licensed for small installations should not be signing off a commercial distribution system.

## 2. Ask who will actually be on site

The company that signs the contract is not always the team that does the work. Ask:

- Who is the responsible engineer or supervisor for this project, and how often will they be on site?
- Will any part of the work be subcontracted? If so, to whom?
- Who signs the test certificate at the end?

A contractor who answers these clearly, by name, usually runs a site the same way.

## 3. Look at comparable work, not just a portfolio

Photographs of finished interiors say little about the electrical work behind the walls. Ask for projects of a similar size and type to yours — a hotel, an office fit-out, an apartment block — and, where possible, a reference you can call. Ask the reference whether the work finished on programme, whether the as-built drawings and test results were handed over, and how defects were dealt with.

## 4. Read what the quotation includes

Two quotations for the "same" job can describe very different work. A useful quotation states:

- the scope, against the drawings or a written specification;
- the main materials and brands — cable, distribution boards, protective devices;
- testing and certification as a line item, not an assumption;
- what happens with variations, and how they are priced;
- the workmanship warranty and its length;
- the programme, and what the contractor needs from others to keep to it.

A much lower price usually means something on that list is missing.

## 5. Coordination matters as much as workmanship

On a building project the electrical installation shares routes with security cabling, solar, standby power and lifts, and its programme depends on the structure and finishes. A contractor used to working with architects and main contractors will ask early for drawings, raise clashes before they are built in, and attend site meetings. One that is not will find the clashes when the plaster is already up.

## 6. Insist on testing and documentation at handover

Every new installation should be inspected and tested before it is energised and handed over, with the results recorded on a test certificate signed by the licensed contractor. You should also receive as-built drawings and distribution board schedules. Without them, every future alteration starts with guesswork. See [what testing and commissioning involves](/insights/understanding-electrical-testing-and-commissioning).

## A short checklist

- EPRA licence, at a class suitable for the job
- NCA registration, in an appropriate category, for construction projects
- Named supervisor and a clear answer on subcontracting
- Comparable projects and at least one reference
- Itemised quotation including testing, certification and warranty
- Agreed handover documents: test certificate, as-built drawings, board schedules
